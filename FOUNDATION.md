## Part One — The Beginning: Seeing Before Knowing

John Ernest Carter has been observing anomalous phenomena his entire life.

This fact carries a specific scientific significance that is easy to miss and essential not to.

He was seeing things before he had any cultural framework to place them in. Before the UAP community existed in its current form. Before the vocabulary had standardized. Before media coverage had saturated the cultural imagination with templates for what anomalous phenomena were supposed to look like.

This matters because expectation shapes perception. A researcher who enters a field already saturated with community imagery, media influence, and academic theory is observing through a filter whether they know it or not. Their pattern recognition is pre-loaded.

John Ernest Carter's earliest observations were made without that filter. What he saw, he saw clean. No template. No community consensus to match against. No incentive to see something specific.

This is the cleanest possible observational condition for anomalous phenomena research. It is rarer than it sounds and more valuable than it is typically credited.

He has been carrying the presignal his whole life. The research program is not a new direction. It is the formal architecture built around something that was always already there.

## Part Two — The Work: 5,000 Hours in the Dark

The subtraction methodology was not invented at a desk. It was built through approximately 5,000 hours or more of direct field work and video analysis conducted over four or more years.

The process was this:

First, go into the field. Shoot footage. GoPro in daylight. SiOnyx Pro at night. Document what the equipment captures.

Then spend the hours. Frame by frame. Cross-referencing every anomalous visual against a self-constructed catalog of known prosaic explanations — bugs, bats, birds, lens artifacts, atmospheric distortion, compression errors, motion blur, sensor noise, infrared reflection, insect wing diffraction, airborne particulate, and dozens of other categories developed through direct reproduction.

The reproduction step is critical and almost nobody does it properly.

John Ernest Carter did not just theorize what bugs look like on a SiOnyx Pro at night. He went out and filmed bugs on a SiOnyx Pro at night, deliberately, systematically, under controlled conditions, until he knew exactly what every category of prosaic phenomenon looks like on every piece of equipment he uses. He built the elimination catalog from primary data, not assumption.

Then he cross-referenced his own field footage against the massive online UAP community — tens of thousands of clips, submitted by thousands of observers, from every kind of equipment, under every kind of condition.

The result of that cross-referencing was the four-category elimination framework:

- **Category I** — Prosaic. Explained by known natural or man-made phenomena.
- **Category II** — Concealed or chronic. Known but not publicly acknowledged.
- **Category III** — Industry artifact. Equipment, military, or infrastructure-related.
- **Category IV** — Genuine anomaly. Survives full subtraction against all known categories.

Category IV is rare. That is the point. The rarity is what gives it meaning. A methodology that produces frequent Category IV results is a methodology with insufficient elimination criteria. John Ernest Carter's methodology is deliberately hard to pass. Most things do not pass it. The things that do are genuinely unexplained.

This is not a methodology that was designed to find anomalies. It is a methodology designed to eliminate them — and it takes seriously what remains when elimination is complete.

## Part Three — The Teaching: One Hour at a Time

While this work was being conducted privately, John Ernest Carter was also teaching it.

Not in academic institutions. Not with credentials or a curriculum or a program. In real rooms, with real people, in one-hour sessions. Teaching the actual critical thinking framework he had developed through field work. Teaching people how to look at UAP footage and think about it properly. Teaching the elimination process. Teaching what prosaic phenomena actually look like on actual equipment. Teaching the difference between *I cannot explain this* and *this is unexplainable*.

Those are not the same thing. Most people treat them as synonyms. They are not.

*I cannot explain this* is a statement about the limits of the observer's knowledge. It is almost always the correct interpretation of an anomalous observation, because most observers do not have the specialized knowledge required to eliminate all prosaic categories.

*This is unexplainable* is a statement about the limits of all known knowledge applied exhaustively. It requires the elimination work to have actually been done. It requires the catalog. It requires the reproduction. It requires the hours.

John Ernest Carter taught people the difference between these two statements. That teaching was real. It was grounded in real experience. It produced real changes in how people evaluated UAP evidence.

And here is what that teaching always circled back to:

The knowledge of the perceiver determines what they see.

A person who does not know what bugs look like on a SiOnyx Pro at night will see something otherworldly when bugs appear in their footage. They are not lying. They are not stupid. They simply do not have the elimination catalog built yet. Their perception is real. Their interpretation is shaped by what they do not know.

This principle — that the knowledge of the perceiver is a primary variable in the interpretation of anomalous phenomena — is one of the most underappreciated facts in the entire field. It has implications not just for UAP research but for every domain where human observation is the primary instrument.

The subtraction methodology corrects for it systematically.

## Part Four — The Rejection: Subtraction as a Way of Being

There is another dimension to the subtraction methodology that does not appear in the academic preprints.

John Ernest Carter has been subtracting his whole life. Not just in field work. Not just in video analysis. In every room he has ever been in where he did not fit. In every social context where the explanation being offered for who he was and what he saw did not match what he actually experienced.

Most people respond to that kind of chronic misfit by adapting. By accepting the explanation that is being offered even when it is wrong. By fitting themselves to the frame rather than questioning the frame.

He did not do that. He kept subtracting. Every explanation that did not hold up was eliminated. What remained after full subtraction — of social expectation, of institutional consensus, of community pressure, of academic authority — was what was actually true.

This is not a metaphor for the scientific methodology. It is the same process. The same discipline applied to a different domain. The consistency of the output — valid findings that survive scrutiny — across both domains is not a coincidence. It is evidence that the methodology is real and that it works regardless of what it is applied to.

Rejection was not an obstacle to the work. Rejection was part of the methodology. Every time an explanation was offered and John Ernest Carter could not make it hold up against what he actually observed, that was the methodology working correctly. The rejection of bad explanations is how good ones eventually emerge.

He has been doing this his whole life. The research program is the formal record of what that discipline produces.
