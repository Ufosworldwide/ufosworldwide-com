Ride · 01

The Signal
and the Noise

Every anomalous observation arrives as a mixture. Signal and noise in unknown proportion. The untrained perceiver cannot separate them. The trained one knows that separation is not a feeling — it is a process. This ride tests whether you understand the process before you apply it.

The Challenge

Part One — Define the Terms

Before any elimination work begins, the terms must be precise. Vague definitions produce vague eliminations. Vague eliminations produce false Category IV results — the most dangerous error in the subtraction methodology.

Define signal. Define noise. Not generally. Define them as they apply specifically to anomalous phenomena research. Your definition must be tight enough to make a decision with.

Part Two — The Three Observations

Below are three observations from the field record. For each one, identify the noise components and the potential signal components. Do not yet eliminate. Only separate.

OBSERVATION A Equipment: SiOnyx Pro · Night field session · Ontario Duration of event: approximately 4 seconds Description: Fast-moving object, no nav lights, no sound, trajectory inconsistent with known flight paths. Observer knowledge at time of sighting: intermediate. Wind: 12 km/h from northwest. Temperature: 6°C. Insects: unlikely at temperature. Bats: possible.
OBSERVATION B Equipment: GoPro Hero · Daylight · Clear sky Duration of event: less than 1 second Description: Small dark object crossing frame at high speed. No sound. Shape indeterminate due to motion blur. Observer knowledge at time of sighting: novice. Altitude estimate: impossible to determine.
OBSERVATION C Equipment: Fixed surveillance camera · Infrared mode Duration of event: 11 seconds Description: Object maintains stationary position for 8 seconds then accelerates to off-frame in under 1 second. No known aircraft exhibits that acceleration profile. Observer knowledge at time of sighting: advanced. Cross-referenced against ADS-B: no registered traffic.

Part Three — The Perceiver Variable

Observer knowledge is listed for each observation above. Explain precisely how the observer's knowledge level changes what counts as noise and what counts as potential signal in each case. This is not a rhetorical question. Give specific examples of what a novice misses that an advanced observer catches — and vice versa.

What a passing answer looks like: Precise definitions that could be used as operational criteria. Separation work on all three observations that shows genuine understanding of the elimination process — not pattern matching, not intuition, not "it looks strange therefore signal." Process. Every step visible.

What a failing answer looks like: conclusions without elimination steps. "Observation C is likely genuine because it accelerated fast." That is a perception, not a finding. The methodology demands more.
